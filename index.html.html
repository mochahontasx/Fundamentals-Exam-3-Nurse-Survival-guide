❤️ Chapter 24 — Communication

Potter & Perry 12th Ed. | The #1 clinical skill — poor communication = sentinel events

⚡ NURSING PRIORITIES — CH. 24

  • Use open-ended questions to allow patient expression — NEVER closed yes/no first
  • Sit DOWN at patient level — never stand over them (power imbalance)
  • SILENCE is a therapeutic technique — let patients process
  • Nonverbal = 55–93% of emotional meaning — watch body language!
  • SBAR for all handoffs — Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation
  • Recognize non-therapeutic responses and NEVER use them on NCLEX

🔊 Levels of Communication

  • Intrapersonal — self-talk, inner dialogue (reflection)
  • Interpersonal — face-to-face between 2 people (MOST COMMON in nursing)
  • Small-group — team meetings, family conferences
  • Public — large audience education (health fairs)
  • Electronic — EHR, telehealth, email (HIPAA applies!)

💬 Verbal Communication

  • Denotative — literal dictionary meaning (e.g., "code")
  • Connotative — implied/emotional meaning (e.g., "code" to patient = death)
  • Pace/Intonation — fast pace = anxiety; slow = calmness or sadness
  • Clarity — simple language, ≤6th grade reading level
  • Timing — patient in pain = not the time for education
  • Relevance — make it matter to the patient's situation

🙅 Nonverbal Communication (55–93%!)

  • Appearance — clean, professional attire = trust
  • Posture — open = receptive; closed arms = defensive
  • Facial expression — must match words (congruence)
  • Eye contact — intermittent = engaged (not staring)
  • Gestures — nod = active listening
  • Proxemics — personal space zones (see below)
  • Touch — therapeutic if patient receptive; cultural variation

📏 Personal Space Zones (Hall's Proxemics)

  • Intimate — 0 to 18 inches (nursing care zone)
  • Personal — 18 in to 4 feet (conversation zone)
  • Social — 4 to 12 feet (group interactions)
  • Public — beyond 12 feet (lectures, presentations)
⚠️Cultural norms vary! Always assess comfort level.

✅ Therapeutic Techniques (RIGHT answers)

  • Active listening — SURETY model (see mnemonic)
  • Silence — allows patient to process thoughts
  • Open-ended questions — "Tell me more about your pain"
  • Paraphrasing — restating in your own words to clarify
  • Clarifying — "I'm not sure I understand, can you explain?"
  • Focusing — redirect to the relevant topic
  • Sharing empathy — "It must be very frustrating..."
  • Sharing observations — "You seem tense today"
  • Touch — when appropriate and patient accepts
  • Summarizing — condenses key themes at end
  • Sharing hope — "I've seen your strength..."

❌ Non-Therapeutic Techniques (WRONG on NCLEX)

  • False reassurance — "Everything will be fine" ← NEVER
  • Giving personal opinions — "I think you should..." ← NEVER
  • Sympathy (not empathy) — "I know how you feel" ← WRONG
  • Changing subject — avoids patient's real concerns
  • Automatic responses — "How are you?" without listening
  • Arguing/defending — never argue with patient
  • Probing — pushing patient beyond readiness
  • Stereotyping — assuming based on group membership
  • Closed-ended Qs — yes/no limits expression

🤝 Nurse-Patient Relationship Phases

  • Pre-interaction — review chart, plan before entering
  • Orientation — introduce self, establish trust, set goals
  • Working — actual therapeutic work happens here
  • Termination — discharge, transfer; acknowledge end of relationship

Peplau's Theory of Interpersonal Relations — foundational for therapeutic relationships

🌍 Factors Affecting Communication

  • Development — age-appropriate language always
  • Gender — communication styles differ; avoid assumptions
  • Culture — eye contact, touch, time orientation vary
  • Emotions — anxiety, pain, fear = barriers
  • Health literacy — 43% US adults = basic/below-basic
  • Environment — noise, privacy = major barriers
  • Physical impairment — vision/hearing/speech deficits

🚨 CRITICAL NCLEX COMMUNICATION RULES

  • Patient says "I want to die" → ASSESS FIRST: "Tell me more about that feeling" — NEVER dismiss or immediately call doctor
  • EMPATHY ≠ SYMPATHY: Empathy = "I understand this must be hard for you" (therapeutic). Sympathy = "I feel so sorry for you" (non-therapeutic)
  • When patient asks about diagnosis → redirect to MD but acknowledge feelings: "That's something to discuss with your doctor. How are you feeling about it?"
  • SBAR is THE professional communication tool — use it for all handoffs, critical values, physician calls
  • Interpreter required for non-English speakers — NEVER use family members if official interpreter available (confidentiality + accuracy)

🧠 SURETY Model — Active Listening

Read top to bottom — every letter = one action!

S

Sit at an angle facing the patient

Non-confrontational positioning. Angle = I'm here to listen, not interrogate. NEVER stand over patient!

U

Uncross your legs and arms

Open posture = open mind. Crossed arms = defensive, closed off — patient picks up on this instantly!

R

Relax

Communicate ease and comfort. Restlessness = you have somewhere better to be. Slow your breathing.

E

Eye contact (intermittent)

Intermittent eye contact = engaged & present. Constant staring = creepy. No eye contact = not interested.

T

Touch (therapeutic)

Respectful, appropriate touch communicates empathy. Always assess patient comfort — cultural variation!

Y

Your intuition

Trust your gut as you grow. Adapt techniques to each unique patient. Intuition = clinical wisdom building.

📢 SBAR — Professional Communication

S

Situation

Identify yourself + patient + what's happening RIGHT NOW. "This is Nurse Sam, Room 412, Mr. Jones is having chest pain."

B

Background

Relevant clinical history: admitting dx, current meds, recent labs, relevant past history.

A

Assessment

YOUR nursing clinical impression: "I believe this may be cardiac in origin based on..."

R

Recommendation

What you need: "I need you to come evaluate this patient" or "Can we get an EKG and troponins?"

1. A patient says, "I feel like no one cares if I live or die." What is the nurse's BEST initial response?
A) "I'm sure your family cares about you very much."
B) "Tell me more about what you're feeling right now."
C) "You shouldn't feel that way. Things will get better."
D) "Have you talked to the social worker about this?"
✅ B is correct. "Tell me more" is an open-ended statement that uses the therapeutic technique of exploring — it encourages expression and shows genuine interest. A is false reassurance (wrong). C dismisses the patient's feelings (wrong). D deflects rather than addressing the patient's emotional state first.
2. Which nursing action BEST demonstrates active listening during a patient interview?
A) Standing at the foot of the bed while asking questions
B) Completing the intake form while the patient speaks
C) Sitting at an angle to the patient with uncrossed arms and intermittent eye contact
D) Nodding and saying "yes" after every sentence the patient speaks
✅ C is correct. This describes the SURETY model — Sit at angle, Uncross arms, intermittent Eye contact = active listening. A (standing) creates power imbalance. B (documenting during interview) shows divided attention. D (constant nodding) is an automatic response, not genuine engagement.
3. A nurse calls a physician about a deteriorating patient. Which communication framework should the nurse use?
A) HIPAA
B) SBAR
C) SURETY
D) FACCO
✅ B is correct. SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) is THE standardized framework for nurse-physician communication during clinical escalation. It reduces miscommunication and ensures critical information is conveyed efficiently. SURETY is active listening. FACCO is documentation quality. HIPAA is privacy law.
4. A patient who speaks limited English is being discharged. The patient's daughter offers to translate. What should the nurse do FIRST?
A) Accept the daughter's offer since she knows her mother's language
B) Request a professional medical interpreter
C) Use simple gestures and pictures to communicate
D) Ask the daughter to translate only non-medical information
✅ B is correct. Professional medical interpreters ensure accuracy, confidentiality, and compliance. Family members may omit, distort, or filter information (especially sensitive medical details). Using family interpreters also violates the patient's right to privacy — they may not want family to know certain information. This is a JCAHO and federal requirement.
5. Which statement by the nurse is an example of sharing empathy? (NGN-style)
A) "I know exactly how you feel — I've been through something similar."
B) "Don't worry, most people with your diagnosis do very well."
C) "It must be frightening to face this diagnosis. I'm here with you."
D) "Let's focus on your discharge instructions now."
✅ C is correct. Empathy = acknowledging the patient's reality + communicating understanding + presence. A is false identification ("I know exactly how you feel" = sympathy). B is false reassurance. D changes the subject, avoiding the patient's emotional state.

📚 Chapter 25 — Patient Education

Teaching is a LEGAL nursing responsibility — you are accountable for your patient's knowledge

⚡ NURSING PRIORITIES — CH. 25

  • ASSESS readiness to learn BEFORE teaching — pain/anxiety = barrier
  • Teach-back is the GOLD standard — "Do you understand?" is NOT adequate
  • Affective domain (changing attitudes/beliefs) is the HARDEST to teach
  • Psychomotor skills REQUIRE return demonstration — never just verbal
  • Use ≤6th grade reading level for all written materials
  • Document teaching AND patient response — legal obligation

🎯 Prevention Levels

  • Primary — Prevent disease before it occurs
    Example: Vaccines, handwashing education, seatbelts
  • Secondary — Early detection + prompt treatment
    Example: Mammograms, BP screening, colonoscopies
  • Tertiary — Rehabilitation, maximize function, prevent complications
    Example: Cardiac rehab, diabetic foot care education

🧠 Bloom's Taxonomy — 3 Learning Domains

  • Cognitive — KNOWING (facts, information)
    Teaching: lecture, discussion, reading materials
  • Affective — FEELING/BELIEVING (values, attitudes)
    Hardest to change! Role play, values clarification
  • Psychomotor — DOING (skills, procedures)
    MUST have return demo! Practice + demonstration
🚨Affective = hardest! Psychomotor = requires return demo, ALWAYS!

📋 Readiness to Learn (4 Types)

  • Physical — free from pain, fatigue, discomfort
  • Emotional — accepting diagnosis, not in denial
  • Experiential — prior experience with topic
  • Knowledge — baseline understanding of subject

NCLEX TIP: If patient is in denial or extremely anxious → address emotional needs BEFORE teaching!

👤 Andragogy — Adult Learning (Knowles)

  • Adults are self-directed — involve them in planning
  • Adults bring prior experience — build on it
  • Adults need to know WHY before they'll learn the how
  • Adults are problem-centered — make it relevant NOW
  • Adults are internally motivated — intrinsic > extrinsic
  • Best time to learn: when they see a need

🔄 Stages of Change (Prochaska TTM)

  • Precontemplation — "I don't have a problem" (not ready)
  • Contemplation — "Maybe I should change..." (thinking)
  • Preparation — Making a plan, getting ready
  • Action — Actively changing behavior
  • Maintenance — Sustaining the change (6+ months)
  • Relapse — Normal! Return to earlier stage

TIP: Match your teaching to the patient's current stage!

✅ Teach-Back Method

  • Gold standard for verifying learning
  • "In your own words, can you tell me how you'll take this medication?"
  • Tests the nurse's teaching, not the patient's intelligence
  • WRONG: "Do you understand?" → yes/no answer is meaningless
  • If patient cannot demonstrate: re-teach using different method
  • Document: what was taught, method used, patient response, need for re-teaching

⛔ Barriers to Learning

  • Pain/fatigue — physical barriers (address first)
  • Anxiety/fear — emotional barriers (reassure first)
  • Denial — "I don't need this info, I'm fine"
  • Language barrier — use certified interpreter
  • Low health literacy — 43% of US adults!
  • Cultural beliefs — may conflict with medical advice
  • Cognitive impairment — involve caregiver

📝 Teaching Methods by Domain

  • Cognitive: Lecture, discussion, pamphlets, videos, demonstrations
  • Affective: Role play, group discussion, values clarification, storytelling
  • Psychomotor: Demonstration → Return demo → Supervised practice
💡Use teach-back for ALL domains. Return demo = ONLY way to verify psychomotor learning!

🧠 Teaching Priority Order — SNNT

S

Safety-critical information FIRST

Teach what keeps the patient alive: medication side effects, when to call 911, fall prevention, allergic reactions

N

Need-to-know information

Core disease management: medication schedule, wound care steps, diet restrictions, activity limits

N

Nice-to-know information

Supplemental info: community resources, support groups, long-term lifestyle modifications

T

Teach-back to verify!

"In your own words, tell me the 3 signs that you should call your doctor immediately." — NEVER "Do you understand?"

🎓 Bloom's Memory Trick — "CAN"

C

Knowing facts, recalling info, understanding concepts → tested with verbal questions

A

Changing values/beliefs/attitudes — the HARDEST domain. Requires time, trust, repeated exposure

N

Physical skills → MUST demonstrate, MUST practice. Return demo = ONLY valid assessment!

🎯 NCLEX Practice — Ch. 25 Patient Education

1. A nurse is teaching insulin injection technique to a newly diagnosed diabetic patient. Which method BEST evaluates the patient's learning?
A) Asking the patient if they understood the demonstration
B) Providing written instructions for the patient to review at home
C) Having the patient perform an independent return demonstration
D) Quizzing the patient verbally about injection sites
✅ C is correct. Insulin injection is a psychomotor skill — the ONLY valid way to verify psychomotor learning is return demonstration. Verbal quizzing (D) tests cognitive knowledge but not the skill itself. "Did you understand?" (A) is never adequate. Written materials (B) are supplemental, not evaluative.
2. A patient newly diagnosed with hypertension says, "I don't believe I have high blood pressure — I feel perfectly fine." Which nursing action is PRIORITY?
A) Begin teaching about the DASH diet and sodium restriction
B) Provide a pamphlet about hypertension complications
C) Explore the patient's understanding and feelings about the diagnosis
D) Explain the purpose of the prescribed antihypertensive medication
✅ C is correct. This patient is in the precontemplation/denial stage (Stages of Change). Teaching is ineffective when the patient is not emotionally ready. The nurse must first address the emotional/affective barrier — explore feelings and understanding — before beginning any formal education. Assessment comes before intervention!
3. After teaching a patient about warfarin therapy, the nurse asks, "In your own words, what foods should you avoid?" This is an example of:
A) Affective domain evaluation
B) Cognitive domain instruction
C) Teach-back method to evaluate learning
D) Return demonstration of a psychomotor skill
✅ C is correct. Teach-back involves asking the patient to explain information in their own words — the gold standard for evaluating learning. It's specifically used for cognitive/knowledge content. Return demonstration (D) is for psychomotor skills. This is not instruction (B) or affective evaluation (A).

📋 Chapter 26 — Informatics & Documentation

"If it wasn't documented, it wasn't done" — The golden legal rule of nursing

⚡ NURSING PRIORITIES — CH. 26

  • Document objectively — facts only, NO opinions or inferences
  • NEVER use TJC "Do Not Use" abbreviations (U, IU, QD, trailing zeros)
  • Incident reports are INTERNAL — NEVER referenced in medical record
  • HIPAA: minimum necessary principle — share only what's needed
  • Telephone orders: Write → Read back verbatim → Confirm → Document T.O.
  • EHR CPOE reduces medication errors 55–85%!

⭐ FACCO — Quality Documentation

  • Factual — objective data only, no interpretations
  • Accurate — precise measurements, correct times
  • Current — timely, near real-time documentation
  • Complete — all relevant data included
  • Organized — logical sequence, follows nursing process
✏️Good: "BP 148/92, patient grimacing." Bad: "Patient seems hypertensive and in pain."

💻 EHR Benefits & Risks

  • Benefits: Legible, accessible, CPOE (reduces errors 55–85%), CDSS alerts (drug interactions, sepsis)
  • Alert fatigue — too many alerts → nurses/MDs click through → CRITICAL ones missed
  • CPOE — computerized provider order entry — orders go directly to pharmacy
  • CDSS — clinical decision support system — allergy alerts, dosing, sepsis bundles
  • Disadvantage — downtime, cyber breaches, copy-paste errors

📞 Telephone/Verbal Orders

  1. Write down the order
  2. Read back the ENTIRE order verbatim
  3. Receive confirmation from prescriber
  4. Document: order + "T.O./Dr. Smith/Read-back confirmed"
  5. MD must co-sign within timeframe (per facility policy)
🚨NEVER act on a verbal order without reading it back first!

📝 Documentation Methods

  • SOAP — Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan
  • DAR — Data, Action, Response (Focus charting)
  • PIE — Problem, Intervention, Evaluation
  • CBE — Charting by Exception (only abnormals)
  • Narrative — flowing paragraph format
  • Flow sheets — VS, I&O, medication administration records (MAR)

🔒 HIPAA Key Rules

  • Minimum necessary — share only what's needed for care
  • Patient has right to ACCESS own records
  • Spouse/family = NO automatic right to PHI without patient consent
  • NO social media posts — even without patient name (identifiable from context)
  • Do not discuss patient info in elevators, hallways, cafeteria
  • Computer screens — lock when leaving; don't leave records open

📄 Incident Reports (Occurrence Reports)

  • Filed for: falls, medication errors, equipment failure, patient injury
  • INTERNAL quality document ONLY — protected from legal discovery
  • NEVER referenced in the medical record — do NOT write "incident report filed"
  • Document the event factually in the medical record (what happened, patient response, interventions)
  • Report up chain of command immediately

🏥 Specialty Documentation Tools

  • MDS — Minimum Data Set — Long-term care, federally mandated, drives Medicare/Medicaid reimbursement
  • OASIS — Home health, comprehensive assessment tool, drives reimbursement
  • Acuity systems — determine nurse-patient ratios
  • Critical pathways — multidisciplinary care plans for specific DRGs

B) Notify the supervisor only after consulting with the physician
C) Assess the patient, document the event in the medical record, and complete an incident report separately

✅ C is correct. Priority 1 = assess patient safety. Then document the clinical facts in the medical record (what happened, patient response, interventions). The patient must have authorized the husband to receive information (healthcare proxy, HIPAA authorization form). Marital status = no automatic right to medical records. The nurse must protect patient privacy regardless of relationship.

🔬 Chapter 30 — Health Assessment & Physical Examination

Potter & Perry 12th Ed. | "You can't treat what you don't assess" — every clinical decision starts HERE

⚡ NURSING PRIORITIES — CH. 30

  • ABDOMEN EXCEPTION: Inspection → Auscultation → Percussion → Palpation (NOT IPPA)
  • Diaphragm = high-pitched sounds; Bell = low-pitched (press LIGHTLY for bell)
  • S3 in adult >40 = left ventricular failure; S4 = ALWAYS pathological
  • Stridor = upper airway emergency — act IMMEDIATELY
  • GCS <8 = severe TBI; intubate. Fixed dilated pupil = herniation = EMERGENCY
  • Positive Babinski in adult = upper motor neuron lesion

🔍 4 Assessment Techniques — IPPA

  • Inspection — systematic visual observation (always FIRST)
  • Palpation — touch to assess texture, temp, masses, tenderness
  • Percussion — tapping to assess density beneath
  • Auscultation — listening with stethoscope

🏃 Chapter 38 — Activity & Exercise

Safe Patient Handling · Mobility Assessment · Assistive Devices · Exercise Therapy

⚡ NURSING PRIORITIES — CH. 38

  • SPHM rule: NEVER manually lift >35 lbs — always use mechanical lift
  • STOP exercise if: HR >20bpm above baseline, SpO2 <90%, chest pain, severe dyspnea
  • Cane goes on the STRONG side; walker = advance first, then step in
  • Crutches = weight on HANDS not axillae (axillary pressure = brachial plexus injury)
  • If patient starts to fall: guide to floor — NEVER catch and hold upright
  • Dangle 1–2 minutes before standing to prevent orthostatic hypotension

⚖️ Body Mechanics Principles

  • Body alignment — proper anatomical relationship reduces strain
  • Center of gravity — at pelvis; lower = more stable (bend knees!)
  • Base of support — wider base = more stability (feet apart)
  • Friction — opposes movement; use slide sheets to reduce
  • Shear — tangential force + friction = pressure injury mechanism
  • Proprioception — body's awareness of position in space (impaired in neuropathy, stroke)

🏋️ Types of Exercise

  • Isotonic — muscle contracts WITH movement/joint motion (walking, swimming, lifting). Improves endurance and strength.
  • Isometric — muscle contraction WITHOUT joint movement (quad sets, pushing against wall). Useful for immobilized patients.
  • Isokinetic — consistent speed of movement (resistance machines)
  • Aerobic — sustained activity using large muscles, improves CV fitness
  • Anaerobic — high intensity, short bursts (sprinting)

🦽 Range of Motion

  • Active ROM — patient performs independently (best — maintains strength)
  • Active-Assistive — patient + nurse assistance
  • Passive ROM — nurse performs for patient (no muscle strengthening)

Passive ROM: minimum 2x/day for dependent patients. Move ALL joints through full ROM. NEVER force a joint — stop at resistance/pain. Support proximal and distal to joint.

🚶 Assistive Devices — Safety Rules

  • Walker — maximum stability. Advance walker 6 inches → step in with WEAKER leg → bring STRONG leg level. 4-point contact always.
  • Cane — on STRONG side (bear weight on cane + weak leg together). Sequence: advance cane + weak leg simultaneously → then strong leg. Height = greater trochanter.
  • Crutches — weight on HANDS, not axillae! 2–3 finger widths below axilla. Crutch palsy = brachial plexus compression.

🩼 Crutch Gaits

  • 4-point gait — R crutch → L foot → L crutch → R foot. MOST STABLE. Weight-bearing both legs.
  • 2-point gait — R crutch + L foot → L crutch + R foot. Faster, less stable.
  • 3-point gait — Both crutches + weak leg → strong leg. NON-WEIGHT-BEARING on weak leg.
  • Swing-through — both crutches → swing body past. Fastest, for paraplegia.

📊 Activity Tolerance Assessment

  • STOP activity if:
  • HR >20 bpm above resting baseline
  • SpO2 <90% (or drop >5%)
  • Chest pain or pressure
  • Severe dyspnea
  • Pallor, diaphoresis, cyanosis
  • Dizziness, syncope, confusion
  • Patient requests to stop

🏥 Progressive Mobilization Protocol

  1. Passive ROM in bed
  2. Dangle at bedside 1–2 min (assess for orthostasis)
  3. Stand at bedside
  4. Chair (geri-chair or wheelchair)
  5. Ambulate in room
  6. Ambulate in hallway

WHY dangle first? Orthostatic hypotension = BP drop ≥20 mmHg systolic within 3 min of standing. Dangling allows cardiovascular adjustment!

💪 SPHM — Safe Patient Handling & Mobility

  • NEVER manually lift a patient >35 lbs of their body weight
  • Use ceiling lifts, Hoyer lifts, slide boards, gait belts
  • BMAT 2.0 (Barnes Mobility Assessment Tool) — assesses mobility level
  • Gait belt — placed at waist, nurse grasps from behind/side. NEVER pull on arms.
  • Lateral transfer from bed to stretcher → use friction-reducing device
  • If patient starts to FALL → guide to floor in controlled descent (protect head)

🪑 Positioning Guidelines

  • Supine — pillow under lower legs (NOT under knees — popliteal compression → DVT). Footboard to prevent foot drop.
  • Lateral (side-lying) — 30° tilt (NOT 90°). Pillow BETWEEN knees. Pillow under dependent arm.

  • Semi-Fowler's — HOB 30°
  • Fowler's — HOB 45–60°
  • High Fowler's — HOB 90° (most upright — max lung expansion)
  • Orthopneic — sitting, leaning over bedside table = severe dyspnea, COPD

🩺 DVT Prevention & Recognition

  • S/S DVT: calf pain, warmth, erythema, swelling (unilateral)
  • Homan's sign — calf pain on dorsiflexion (NOT reliable, NOT used now)
  • Prevention: SCDs (sequential compression devices) — remove only during ambulation; Ted hose; early ambulation; anticoagulants (heparin SQ)
  • If DVT suspected: DO NOT massage calf; notify MD; expect Doppler ultrasound; elevate extremity, apply warm compress
  • PE signs: sudden chest pain, dyspnea, tachycardia, hypoxia → EMERGENCY

🦷 Contracture Prevention

  • Passive ROM minimum 2x/day — all joints through full range
  • Foot drop — foot not plantar flexed → fixed plantar flexion. Prevent: footboard, high-tops, AFO splints, dorsiflexion position
  • Hand contracture — use resting splints for hand/wrist alignment
  • Hip/knee contracture — avoid prolonged flexion; prone positioning when tolerated
  • Muscle atrophy — isometric exercises even in bed help!

  • Negative nitrogen balance — muscle catabolism exceeds anabolism. Causes: inadequate protein, immobility, illness

  • Nursing: High protein (1.25–1.5g/kg/day), vitamin C, zinc, 2–3L fluids

Without oxygen, death in 4–6 minutes. This chapter = the most life-saving content in nursing school.

⚡ NURSING PRIORITIES — CH. 41

  • AIRWAY first — ALWAYS. A patient can't breathe anything else matters less.
  • SpO2 <90% = hypoxia → INTERVENE immediately (apply O2, notify MD)
  • COPD target SpO2: 88–92% (hypoxic drive — high O2 → respiratory arrest!)
  • Right mainstem bronchus = shorter, wider, more vertical → foreign bodies go RIGHT
  • S/S hypoxia: restlessness, confusion, tachycardia, tachypnea, cyanosis (late sign)
  • NEVER leave O2 off for more than 15 sec during suction pass

🫁 Ventilation vs Respiration vs Oxygenation

  • Ventilation — mechanical movement of air IN and OUT of lungs. The PUMP. Assessed by: RR, depth, chest excursion, breath sounds.
  • Respiration — exchange of O2 and CO2 at alveolar-capillary membrane (external) and at cell level (internal).
  • Oxygenation — the combined result: how well cells receive oxygen.
⚠️Patient can VENTILATE (chest moving) but NOT oxygenate (thick secretions, consolidation blocking exchange)! RR alone ≠ oxygenation status. Always check SpO2!

  2. Check PaCO2: >45 = respiratory ACID; <35 = respiratory BASE

🫁 Airway Anatomy Facts

  • Right mainstem bronchus — shorter, wider, more vertical → foreign bodies + misplaced ET tubes go RIGHT preferentially
  • Epiglottis — flap that closes during swallowing to protect airway. Failure → aspiration pneumonia
  • Surfactant — produced by Type II pneumocytes. Reduces surface tension, keeps alveoli open. Premature infants lack it → RDS (respiratory distress syndrome)
  • Cilia — move mucus and particles upward (mucociliary escalator). Damaged by smoking, intubation, infection

💊 Positioning for Oxygenation

  • High Fowler's (90°) — best for breathing in most patients; maximizes lung expansion
  • Orthopneic — leaning over bedside table; COPD, acute dyspnea
  • Lateral (affected side DOWN) — for unilateral pneumonia: good lung gets more perfusion (V/Q matching) when dependent
  • Prone positioning — for ARDS: improves oxygenation by redistributing lung perfusion, opening dorsal alveoli
  • Trendelenburg — NO longer recommended for shock (increases WOB, aspiration risk)

🔬 Suctioning — Key Rules

  • Pre-oxygenate with 100% O2 × 30–60 sec BEFORE suctioning
  • Suction on WITHDRAWAL only (never advance with suction on)
  • Duration: ≤10–15 seconds per pass
  • Allow patient to recover (30–60 sec) between passes
  • Post-suction: re-oxygenate; auscultate for improvement
  • Tracheostomy: obturator at bedside ALWAYS. Cuff 20–25 cmH2O.
  • Signs of need for suction: gurgling, RR ↑, SpO2 ↓, restlessness

🚨 Signs of Hypoxia

  • Early: Restlessness, anxiety, confusion, tachycardia, tachypnea
  • Late: Cyanosis (LATE and UNRELIABLE sign!), bradycardia (pre-arrest), hypotension, stupor
  • ACCESS mnemonic (see below)
  • SpO2 <90% = hypoxia → apply O2 immediately → notify MD
  • Cyanosis can be absent even with significant hypoxia (anemia, dark skin)

🧠 ABG Quick Trick — ROME

R — Respiratory = Opposite

pH and CO2 move in OPPOSITE directions:

  • pH↓ + CO2↑ = Respiratory ACIDOSIS (COPD, pneumonia, drug OD)
  • pH↑ + CO2↓ = Respiratory ALKALOSIS (hyperventilation, anxiety, PE)

ME — Metabolic = Equal (same direction)

pH and HCO3 move in SAME direction:

  • pH↓ + HCO3↓ = Metabolic ACIDOSIS (DKA, renal failure, diarrhea)
  • pH↑ + HCO3↑ = Metabolic ALKALOSIS (vomiting, NG suction, diuretics)

🔪 Debridement Types

  • Sharp — scalpel/scissors. FASTEST. Selective.
  • Enzymatic — collagenase (Santyl). Selective. Good for outpatient.
  • Mechanical — wet-to-dry, irrigation, whirlpool. NON-selective (removes good tissue too).
  • Autolytic — body's own enzymes (occlusive dressings). SLOWEST. Selective. Painless.
  • Biotherapy — medical-grade maggots. Very selective.

📊 Braden Scale — Pressure Injury Risk

  • 6 subscales: Sensory Perception, Moisture, Activity, Mobility, Nutrition, Friction/Shear
  • Scoring: Score ≤18 = AT RISK. Score ≤12 = HIGH RISK.
  • REMEMBER: LOWER Braden score = HIGHER risk (opposite of what you'd think!)
  • Range 6–23. Score of 23 = no risk. Score of 6 = maximum risk.
  • Mnemonic SMANF: Sensory, Moisture, Activity, Nutrition, Friction

🌡️ Heat & Cold Therapy

  • Heat — vasodilation → increased blood flow → promotes healing. Use for: chronic pain, muscle spasm, joint stiffness
  • Cold — vasoconstriction → reduces swelling/bleeding. Use for: ACUTE injury (first 24–48h), inflammation, sprains
  • Check skin q5 min for first 15 min; then q hour
  • Never apply ice/heat DIRECTLY to skin (towel wrap)
  • Remove after 20–30 minutes (rebound vasodilation after)
  • Elderly/impaired sensation = higher burn risk from heat

B) Respiratory alkalosis
C) Respiratory acidosis
D) Metabolic alkalosis

B) Increase oxygen to maintain SpO₂ of 98–100%
C) Continue current monitoring — SpO₂ 88–92% is target for COPD
D) Call the provider immediately
✅ C — COPD patients rely on HYPOXIC DRIVE to breathe (chronically elevated CO₂ makes them insensitive to CO₂ as a drive). Target SpO₂ = 88–92%. Giving too much O₂ can ELIMINATE their hypoxic drive → respiratory arrest. A SpO₂ of 94% in COPD is actually acceptable — monitor, don't increase O₂.

🟠 Knowles' Principles of Adult Learning (Andragogy) ▼
Adults learn differently from children (pedagogy). Key Knowles principles:

1. Self-concept: Adults are self-directed. Involve them in planning their education. Ask: "What do you already know? What would you like to learn first?"

2. Experience: Adults have rich life experience. Build on it. Acknowledge it. Connect new learning to what they already know.

3. Readiness to learn: Adults are ready to learn when it's relevant to their real life. A newly diagnosed diabetic is MORE ready to learn than someone without immediate health needs.

4. Orientation to learning: Adults are problem-centered, not subject-centered. They want practical, applicable knowledge — not abstract theory.

5. Motivation: Internal motivation is more powerful than external. Find out WHY the patient wants to get better.

Always assess what the patient wants to know FIRST, not just what you think they need to know. Patient-identified learning needs = better engagement and outcomes.

🟠 Health Literacy & Teaching Strategies ▼
Health literacy = ability to obtain, process, and understand basic health information to make appropriate decisions.

Low health literacy warning signs: Patient says "I'll read this later," shame about not understanding, medications not taken correctly, frequently misses appointments.

Best Teaching Strategies:
  • Teach-back (gold standard) — "Can you show me how you'll do that at home?"
  • Chunking — teach 3–5 key points per session maximum
  • Plain language — 5th-8th grade reading level for materials
  • Return demonstration — best for psychomotor skills
  • Visual aids — diagrams, anatomical models, videos
  • Written summaries — for cognitive content, to take home
Never ask "Do you understand?" — patients almost always say YES to avoid embarrassment. Always use teach-back or return demonstration instead.

🔷 Legal Issues in Documentation — High NCLEX Priority ▼

  • Never erase or use correction fluid — draw one line through error, write "error," sign/date

  • Document actual time of events (not when you charted them)

Falsifying documentation = license revocation. Adding information after the fact without labeling = fraud. Both are grounds for termination AND legal action.
🔷 Technology in Documentation: EHR, CPOE, CDSS ▼
EHR (Electronic Health Record): Comprehensive digital record accessible to all members of the health team. Reduces errors, improves communication. Nurses document assessments, interventions, medications here.

CPOE (Computerized Provider Order Entry): Providers enter orders directly into the computer → eliminates handwriting errors → alerts generated for drug interactions, allergies, dosing errors.

CDSS (Clinical Decision Support System): AI-based alerts that pop up during order entry — "This dose is higher than recommended" or "Patient is allergic to penicillin." Reduces errors but alert fatigue is a real concern.

Bar-Code Medication Administration (BCMA): Scan patient wristband + medication barcode before giving any medication. Confirms 5 Rights at point of care. Reduces medication errors significantly.

Even with EHR, the nurse is responsible for checking the 5 Rights PERSONALLY before administration. Technology assists — it doesn't replace clinical judgment.

🔵 Safe Patient Handling & Mobility (SPHM) — Full Guide ▼
The Golden Rule: NEVER manually lift more than 35 lbs (16 kg)

BMAT 2.0 — Barnes-Jewish Hospital Mobility Assessment Tool: Assesses patient's highest safe mobility level:
• Level 1: Can tolerate full weight bearing and ambulate
• Level 2: Can partially assist with movement
• Level 3: Requires significant assistance (use mechanical lift)
• Level 4: Totally dependent; full mechanical assist required

Equipment for safe handling:
  • Mechanical lift (Hoyer lift) — for non-weight-bearing or dependent patients
  • Transfer belt (gait belt) — always use for ambulating unsteady patients
  • Slide board — lateral transfers (bed to stretcher)
  • Draw sheet — repositioning in bed
  • Air-assisted transfer device — reduces friction during lateral transfers
Nurses who manually lift heavy patients are the #1 occupational injury risk in healthcare. SPHM = protecting YOURSELF AND the patient. Always use equipment. Never skip because "it's quicker."
🔵 Activity Tolerance — When to STOP Exercise ▼
Stop activity/exercise immediately and return patient to rest if ANY of these occur:

  • 💔 Chest pain or tightness
  • 😰 Severe shortness of breath, accessory muscle use
  • 😵 Dizziness, lightheadedness, syncope
  • 💜 Cyanosis of lips, fingernails
  • 📉 SpO₂ drops below 88–90% with exertion
  • ❤️ HR increases more than 20 bpm above baseline with minimal activity
  • 🩸 Systolic BP drops more than 20 mmHg during activity
  • 😣 Diaphoresis (profuse sweating) unexplained by ambient temperature
  • 🤢 Nausea and vomiting
If a patient complains of chest pain during ambulation — STOP, have patient sit/lie down, assess VS, notify provider. NEVER continue activity through these warning signs.

🧼 Ch.40 — Hygiene: Deep Dive

🩷 Eye, Ear, Nose Special Care — High-Yield Details ▼
EYE CARE:
• Always clean from inner canthus (inner corner) to outer canthus
• Use a SEPARATE part of the cloth or separate cloth for each eye
• NEVER clean across both eyes with the same cloth surface — spreads infection
• Contact lens care: Remove soft lenses daily; store in sterile lens solution, NOT tap water; clean case weekly
• Artificial eye: Remove, cleanse with sterile normal saline, store in labeled container

EAR CARE:
• Hearing aids: Remove before bathing; store in labeled container; clean with dry cloth (NOT wet)
• Check battery regularly; most last 1–2 weeks
• Document that hearing aids are in place or removed — MAJOR fall risk if patient can't hear instructions
• Cotton-tipped applicators: NOT recommended inside ear canal → compacts cerumen

NASAL CARE:
• Blow gently (one nostril at a time) — hard blowing forces bacteria into Eustachian tubes
• For patients with NG tubes: clean nasal care around NG tube insertion site; prevent skin breakdown
ALWAYS document that hearing aids/glasses/dentures were removed and where they are stored. Lost hearing aids/dentures are ENORMOUS patient satisfaction and legal issues.
🩷 Foot Care — Especially for Diabetic Patients ▼
General Foot Care:
• Wash feet DAILY in warm water (110–115°F — test with elbow, not hand)
• Dry THOROUGHLY between toes (moisture = fungal infection)
• Apply moisturizing lotion to heels and soles (NOT between toes)
• Trim nails STRAIGHT ACROSS (not rounded) to prevent ingrown nails

DIABETIC PATIENT FOOT CARE — Special Rules:
• NEVER cut nails of a diabetic patient → must be done by podiatrist
• Check water temperature with thermometer — neuropathy reduces sensation
• Inspect feet DAILY for cuts, blisters, redness (neuropathy = patient won't feel injury)
• NO open-toed shoes, bare feet, or heating pads on feet
• Report ANY wound or color change IMMEDIATELY — diabetics have poor wound healing
• Check between toes for maceration/breakdown
A diabetic patient's small foot wound can lead to gangrene and amputation in days if undetected. Foot inspection is a life-saving nursing intervention.

4-Step ABG Interpretation:
1. Look at pH → acidosis or alkalosis?
2. Look at CO₂ → if CO₂ goes the SAME direction as pH = respiratory cause (OPPOSITE of pH, since CO₂ is acidic)
3. Look at HCO₃ → if HCO₃ goes the SAME direction as pH = metabolic cause
4. Is compensation occurring? (Both CO₂ and HCO₃ moved? = compensated)
ROME: Respiratory = Opposite (CO₂ up = pH down = acidosis; CO₂ down = pH up = alkalosis). Metabolic = Equal (HCO₃ up = pH up = alkalosis; HCO₃ down = pH down = acidosis).

    The cane goes on the STRONG (unaffected) side. Mnemonic: "Cane and Weak leg go TOGETHER" — move cane first, then weak leg, then strong leg.

    Stairs:
    Going UP → Good leg goes FIRST 😇 ("Good goes to Heaven")
    Going DOWN → Cane + bad leg first 😈 ("Bad goes to Hell")

    ⚖️ SPHM Weight Limits

    ⚖️ 35 lbs MAX

    Safe Patient Handling and Mobility (SPHM):
    NEVER manually lift more than 35 lbs (16 kg)

    Why? Nurse back injuries = #1 occupational hazard in healthcare.

    Equipment: Hoyer lift (dependent), gait belt (ambulating), slide board (lateral transfers), draw sheet (repositioning)

    🧠 Visual Atlas Quick Recall — Can You Explain These in 10 Seconds?

    What is Cushing's Triad? ▼
    HTN + Bradycardia + Irregular respirations = ↑ICP → EMERGENCY. Call provider NOW.
    What makes Kwashiorkor different from Marasmus? ▼
    Kwashiorkor = EDEMA (protein-only deficient). Marasmus = NO edema (total starvation — protein AND calories).
    ROME stands for? ▼
    Respiratory Opposite (CO₂↑=pH↓), Metabolic Equal (HCO₃↑=pH↑)
    What does GCS ≤8 mean clinically? ▼
    Severe TBI. Likely needs intubation. "If GCS is 8 or less, intubate!"
    DVT — what do you NEVER do? ▼
    NEVER massage a painful/warm calf. It can dislodge the clot → PE → death. Stop all exercises. Notify provider immediately.
    Abdominal exam order? ▼
    Inspection → Auscultation → Percussion → Palpation (IAPP). Auscultate BEFORE touching — don't alter bowel sounds!
